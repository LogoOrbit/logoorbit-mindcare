The invitation

The five minutes nobody teaches you

A client’s breathing goes shallow. They grip the arms of the chair and tell you they are dying, or their eyes go flat and they are simply no longer in the room with you. Everything you have read about panic and dissociation is true, and none of it tells you what to say in the next sixty seconds.

This workshop is built around exactly that gap. Two hours, live, on triaging acute distress fast, the somatic tools that genuinely settle an activated nervous system, and the scripts to say out loud while you use them — then a guided simulation, so the protocol has been run once before the day you need it.

The Running Order

Two hours, on the clock

Every block earns its place. Assess, then stabilise, then rehearse it under pressure, then learn to talk about it the way a panel wants to hear.

00:00 – 00:30

Rapid Crisis Triage

Before any technique, one question: what is actually happening in front of you? The first half hour is about reading acute distress accurately and fast, inside the Psychological First Aid framework.

  • Telling panic, dissociation and acute distress apart while they are happening
  • Psychological First Aid as a working sequence, not a definition to recite
  • Risk and safety questions you can ask without escalating the room
  • The moment a session stops being therapy and becomes stabilisation
00:30 – 01:15

Core Grounding Protocols

The longest block, and the practical heart of the workshop: the somatic tools that settle an activated nervous system, and the words you say while you use them.

  • Vagal resetting: breath, temperature and orienting, and why each one works
  • Dual-awareness anchoring for dissociation, flashbacks and depersonalisation
  • De-escalation scripts, word for word, for a client who is already activated
  • Pace, posture and tone: what your own body is doing while you do it
01:15 – 01:45

Guided Case Simulation

One acute crisis, walked through step by step in the session rather than described in the abstract, so the protocols have been used once before you need them.

  • A full scenario worked live, from the first sign to the handover
  • Decision points: what you do, what you say, and what you deliberately leave alone
  • Closing a destabilised session safely, and writing up what happened
  • The common mistakes, named plainly, including the well-meant ones
01:45 – 02:00

Clinical Interview Prep & Q&A

Supervisors and hiring panels ask crisis questions on purpose. The last block is about answering them like someone who has done it.

  • The structure a panel is listening for in a crisis-response answer
  • Turning today's protocols into clean two-minute answers
  • What a supervisor is really asking with “what would you do first?”
  • Open Q&A with the facilitator

Straight talk

What this is, and what it is not

Two hours of skills training. It will not qualify anyone to manage a psychiatric emergency alone, it is not a licence, and it does not replace your supervisor, your organisation’s protocols or your local emergency services. Anyone who tells you a short workshop does is selling something.

What it does do is narrow: it turns the first five minutes into something you have practised, with a framework to assess by, tools that work on a body rather than an argument, and the words already chosen.
